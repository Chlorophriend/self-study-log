What the measures are based on

Mood, energy, tension. Momentary affect is usually described on three axes: pleasantness, energetic arousal and tense arousal (Russell, 1980; Thayer, 1989; Wilhelm & Schoebi, 2007). One item each keeps a rating under ten seconds.

When ratings are taken. Ratings are tied to the start and end of work, which is event-based experience sampling. It avoids asking you to remember how a session felt later, and the before/after difference shows what a kind of work does to you.

No preset answer. The scales start blank. A slider with a default position pulls answers toward it.

Estimate ratio. Actual minutes divided by planned minutes for finished tasks. People underestimate their own task time fairly reliably (Buehler, Griffin & Ross, 1994), and the ratio per work type tells you how much to pad.

Mental demand. A single item adapted from the NASA Task Load Index (Hart & Staveland, 1988).

Weekly screen. The PHQ-4 (Kroenke et al., 2009): two anxiety items and two depression items, each scored 0 to 3. A pair scoring 3 or more is the usual cut-off for follow-up. It is a screen and cannot diagnose anything.

Chunks and goal posts. A long task can be split into chunks of the length you choose, each with an optional goal post: a concrete target set before you start. After a chunk you rate how much of the goal post you reached, which measures progress without leaning on mood. Calendar placeholders only hold time; your chunks decide the length.

Expectation and reality. Before a session you predict how demanding it will be and how much progress you will make; afterwards you rate what happened. Forecasts of one's own experience are off in consistent directions (Wilson & Gilbert, 2003), and the gap for each kind of work is something you can plan around.

When the analysis runs. Nothing is scheduled. Every chart and finding is recalculated from your stored entries each time you open Insights, for the range you pick. The 4-day check-up always compares the last 4 days with the 28 days before them, and calls a value out of range when it sits more than one standard deviation from your own average.

Work logged afterwards. A session logged from memory gets one overall rating, with no separate before and after. Remembered feelings drift toward the most intense moment, the ending, and your mood while answering, so a remembered before-and-after pair mostly measures memory (Kahneman et al., 1993; Robinson & Clore, 2002). These entries count fully for time, are marked as recalled, and stay out of rating averages unless you include them on the Insights tab.

Reading the findings. Every comparison shows how many entries it rests on and stays hidden until there are enough. They are correlations within one person. To test one, change that one thing for two weeks and watch the same chart.
